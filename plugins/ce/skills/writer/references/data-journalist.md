# The Data Journalist

For: Data-driven articles, reports and analyses where numbers or charts carry the argument

Contents: voice, numbers, numbers and charts, hedging, attribution, explaining to a layperson, structure and pacing, anti-patterns.

For story structures (ledes, nut grafs, kickers), load the `structuring-articles` skill; this file adds only what data changes.

## Voice

A reporter who works with data, not an analyst who happens to write. The data is the evidence; people and consequences are the subject.

- Lead with what you found, then how.
- Every number earns its place by advancing the story. Cut the rest.
- State the consequence of a finding ("teams below that line missed the launch"), not a directive about it ("that number is the one to sit with").
- Treat uncertainty honestly: say what's known, and name what isn't.

## Numbers

AP style is the baseline: spell out zero through nine, use numerals for 10 and up and always for ages, percentages, dates, money and measurements. Write large numbers as `$8 million`, not `$8,000,000`. Don't start a sentence with a numeral; restructure it.

Round aggressively. "About 4.2 million" beats "4,197,643" unless the precision is the point.

Keep to about 8 to 12 digits per paragraph, dates included. A paragraph over that is either a chart or two paragraphs.

Do the math for the reader. Never give two numbers and leave the difference to them.

```
Bad:  Support costs were $490,000 in 2021 and $635,000 in 2024.
Good: Support costs grew nearly 30 percent in three years, to $635,000.
```

Turn a small, abstract percentage into "1 in X" ("about 1 in 8 customers"), and state it that way once rather than giving the percentage and then revealing the ratio. Use percentages to compare groups or show change, and absolute numbers when the human scale needs to land.

Give every standalone number a comparison: a trend, a baseline, a peer, a per-person figure, or a historical anchor ("the highest since the 2008 pricing change").

## Numbers and charts

A run of figures in prose is a chart the piece hasn't built yet. A paragraph comparing four or more figures across groups, years or places becomes a chart, and the prose shrinks to the claim the chart proves plus one or two anchor numbers. Prose beside a chart states the takeaway and the one number the argument turns on; it doesn't walk through values the reader can see.

```
Bad:  44 percent of revenue comes from contracts over $100,000, 322 deals worth $96 million
      of $218 million. Another 22 percent falls between $25,000 and $100,000...
Good: Nearly half the revenue comes from six-figure contracts.
```

Prose and chart each stand alone. Don't write "as the chart below shows"; state the finding and let the chart prove it. Chart titles label the data ("Monthly active users, 2020 to 2025"); the prose states the finding. Don't send the reader to a chart in another article; rebuild it here.

Give each chart at most three annotations, each resolving a "why?" the reader would otherwise ask. A chart that needs more is telling two stories, so split it. For cost over more than about five years, show an inflation-adjusted series or say why the nominal figure is the point.

## Hedging

Match the verb to the evidence:

| Evidence | Language |
|---|---|
| Randomized or quasi-experimental | "causes," "leads to" |
| Strong observational, with controls | "is associated with," "is linked to" |
| Correlation only | "tracks with," "coincides with" |
| A single snapshot | "in 2024, X was" (a fact, no causal verb) |
| A projection or model | "is projected to," "the model estimates" |

Avoid "proves" for observational findings, and "dramatically," "skyrocketed" and "plummeted" in place of a number. Qualify superlatives ("among the highest") unless you've verified them. "The data suggest" is usually more honest than "the data show." Quantify uncertainty where you can: "declining about 2 percent a year through 2028," not "will keep falling."

## Attribution

Weave sources into the sentence: "according to" for data and reports, "said" for people. Give the full source name first and shorten it after. Keep methodology (cleaning decisions, adjustments) in a methods note, not the narrative.

Every person presented as real (a name, a quote, a scene) traces to a citable source. Never invent, composite or embellish a person as if reported. A clearly signaled hypothetical ("a manager with 30 hours before the review") is fine; the line is whether a reader could mistake it for reporting. Without a sourced person or a fitting hypothetical, write the shorter data-led piece.

## Explaining to a layperson

Write for a smart reader who has never followed the topic, has no training in statistics, finance, law or policy, and hasn't read earlier coverage.

- Define jargon and acronyms inline on first use, in a short clause.
- When the story depends on how something works, explain the mechanism in a sentence or two before building on it. Naming the term doesn't explain it, and a concrete example the reader can picture beats an abstract description.
- Translate every statistic into what it means for the reader's question. No bare correlation coefficients or "per capita" without saying what they show.
- Don't refer to an earlier event, law or controversy as if the reader remembers it; one clause of context is enough.
- When the outcome is known, report it rather than the attempt. Give the consequence along with the mechanism.

This keeps the register of a well-edited magazine feature. If a concept takes more than two or three sentences to explain, a diagram or chart probably does it better.

## Structure and pacing

- Use a data lede when the number provokes "wait, what?" with no setup. If you have to explain why it's interesting, open on a person or scene instead, and land the nut graf by paragraph three to five.
- A section usually runs claim, chart, context, consequence: the reader knows what to look for before the chart appears. Vary the shape; identical machinery in every section reads as generated.
- Put at least two or three sentences of prose between charts. Lead with the strongest chart.
- A section much past 500 words usually holds two findings.
- Close by resolving the opening (return to the person with new information, or show where the number is heading), not by compressing the piece into a one-liner. A closing chart that sums up the argument works when one chart can carry it.

## Anti-patterns

- **The data dump.** More numbers than interpretation in a paragraph. The finding comes first; the numbers support it.
- **Effort as importance.** "After analyzing 1,200 records across 15 years..." The reader wants the finding: "Rural customers churned three times faster, according to an analysis of 15 years of account data."
- **A number that is correct and still misleading.** Raw counts where rates belong, percentage changes off a small base, a window chosen because it shows the effect. If shifting the window or the comparison group flips the finding, say so.
- **No humans.** A story with only numbers needs to be short. If it runs longer, find a sourced person.
